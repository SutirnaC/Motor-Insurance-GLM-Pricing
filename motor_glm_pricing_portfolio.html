Context

Business Context

Insurance Product

This project concerns Private Motor Own Damage (OD) + Third Party Liability (TPL) pricing for a mid-size non-life insurer operating in the Indian market under IRDAI regulation. The product covers personal vehicles (private cars) on an annual policy basis, with premiums charged for OD (comprehensive cover) and a statutory TP component.

In India, TP motor premiums are tariffed by IRDAI and not risk-differentiated at the insurer level. The commercial opportunity — and therefore the entire modelling exercise — lies in OD pricing, where de-tariffication since 2007 allows risk-based pricing. This distinction is critical to mention in any Indian actuarial interview.

Market Situation

The Indian motor insurance market has grown at ~14% CAGR over the past five years, driven by increasing vehicle penetration, IRDAI-mandated long-term policies for new vehicles (3-year TP + 1-year OD or 5-year bundled for two-wheelers), and a gradual shift from agency-driven to digital distribution.

However, the market faces structural pressures: combined ratios above 110% for most private insurers on motor OD, rising spare parts inflation driven by EV transition costs, high repair shop consolidation, and increasing severity from new-age vehicle electronics. Insurers that cannot price risk accurately face adverse selection from aggregators and web-aggregator channels.

Problem Statement

FairDrive General Insurance's motor OD book is showing a loss ratio of 78% (vs a target of 65%), concentrated in the 3–7 year vehicle age band and in metro regions. The current tariff uses only 4 rating factors (vehicle age, engine cc, geography, NCD) and was last updated 18 months ago. Management suspects the book has accumulated adverse risk because newer risks — particularly SUVs and EVs — are underpriced relative to actual cost.

Why Management Wants This Analysis

  • The current flat rating structure does not reflect the true risk relativities revealed by 3 years of accumulated claims data.
  • Web aggregator business has grown to 35% of new business, creating selection pressure: competitors with better models are skimming good risks.
  • The upcoming renewal cycle (April) requires a revised rating manual for agents and digital platforms.
  • The Chief Actuary must satisfy the IRDAI File & Use requirements for any material tariff revision — the GLM provides the statistical evidence base.

Project Objective

Actuarial Question

What is the risk-adequate OD premium for each combination of rating factors in our motor portfolio, and by how much does the current tariff over- or under-price each segment?

Expected Outputs

  1. A Frequency GLM (Poisson, log link) estimating claim frequency per unit of exposure by rating factor
  2. A Severity GLM (Gamma, log link) estimating expected claim cost conditional on a claim occurring
  3. A Pure Premium = Frequency × Severity, providing the indicated technical rate
  4. A Rate Adequacy Analysis comparing indicated to current charged premium by segment
  5. A Validation Report with lift charts, Gini coefficients, and residual diagnostics
  6. A Power BI Monitoring Dashboard for ongoing portfolio management
  7. A Rating Factor Table ready for tariff submission to IRDAI (File & Use)

Data Quality Considerations
  • Exposure accuracy: Mid-term cancellations reduce exposure below 1.0 — confirm exposure is earned, not written.
  • Development lag: Most recent accident year claims are undeveloped. Apply development factors or use only accident years with >90% development.
  • Large loss cap: Truncate claims above ₹5L at modelling stage; model separately or winsorise.
  • NCD endogeneity: NCD is correlated with prior claims history — a risk signal, not just a discount.
  • IRDAI constraints: Gender and religion are prohibited pricing factors under IRDAI guidelines. Include in EDA only, exclude from final model.

Synthetic Data Generation

The synthetic dataset is designed to replicate realistic actuarial data properties: non-linear risk relationships, feature correlations, overdispersion, seasonality, missing values, and deliberate data quality issues for the cleaning exercise. All relationships are grounded in real-world motor insurance behaviour.

# ============================================================
# Motor Insurance GLM Pricing — Synthetic Data Generation
# Author: Actuarial Portfolio Project
# Purpose: Generate realistic motor OD policy + claims data
# ============================================================

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from scipy import stats
import warnings
warnings.filterwarnings('ignore')

# ── Reproducibility ──────────────────────────────────────
np.random.seed(42)
N = 100_000  # 100,000 policy-years across 3 accident years

# ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
# STEP 1: Generate Rating Factors
# Each variable uses distributions reflecting real portfolios
# ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

# Accident year: roughly equal split across 3 years
accident_year = np.random.choice([2021, 2022, 2023],
                                  size=N, p=[0.30, 0.35, 0.35])

# Vehicle age: right-skewed; most policies are on 2–5 year old vehicles
# Modelled as a mixture: new cars (0–2yr) and older fleet (3–15yr)
vehicle_age = np.where(
    np.random.rand(N) < 0.3,
    np.random.randint(0, 3, N),       # 30% new vehicles
    np.random.randint(3, 16, N)        # 70% older vehicles
)

# Vehicle segment: reflects Indian private car market mix
segments = ['Hatchback', 'Sedan', 'SUV', 'Luxury', 'EV']
seg_probs = [0.42, 0.28, 0.20, 0.06, 0.04]
vehicle_segment = np.random.choice(segments, size=N, p=seg_probs)

# IDV (Insured Declared Value) by segment — log-normal
# Mean IDV: Hatchback ₹4L, Sedan ₹8L, SUV ₹14L, Luxury ₹35L, EV ₹18L
idv_params = {'Hatchback': (4.0, 0.35), 'Sedan': (8.0, 0.40),
              'SUV': (14.0, 0.45), 'Luxury': (35.0, 0.55), 'EV': (18.0, 0.30)}
vehicle_value = np.array([
    np.random.lognormal(np.log(idv_params[s][0]), idv_params[s][1])
    for s in vehicle_segment
])
# Depreciate IDV by vehicle age (Indian IDV formula: 5%/yr up to 50%)
depreciation = np.clip(1 - 0.05 * vehicle_age, 0.50, 1.0)
vehicle_value = vehicle_value * depreciation

# Driver age: bimodal — young drivers (18–25) and mature drivers (26–65)
driver_age = np.where(
    np.random.rand(N) < 0.15,
    np.random.randint(18, 26, N),     # 15% young drivers
    np.clip(np.random.normal(42, 12, N).astype(int), 26, 75)
)

# Region: Indian metro / Tier breakdown
regions = ['Metro', 'Tier1', 'Tier2', 'Tier3']
region_probs = [0.35, 0.30, 0.25, 0.10]
region = np.random.choice(regions, size=N, p=region_probs)

# NCD level: 0% (new/claim) to 50% (5+ clean years)
# Distribution skewed to higher NCD — most renewals are clean
ncd_levels = [0, 20, 25, 35, 45, 50]
ncd_probs  = [0.20, 0.12, 0.15, 0.18, 0.20, 0.15]
ncd_level  = np.random.choice(ncd_levels, size=N, p=ncd_probs)

# Channel
channels = ['Agent', 'Broker', 'Digital', 'Bancassurance']
channel_probs = [0.45, 0.15, 0.30, 0.10]
channel = np.random.choice(channels, size=N, p=channel_probs)

# Renewal number (0 = new business)
renewal_number = np.random.choice(range(6), size=N,
                                   p=[0.25, 0.20, 0.18, 0.15, 0.12, 0.10])

# Exposure (earned policy-years): mid-year cancellations reduce below 1.0
exposure = np.where(np.random.rand(N) < 0.05,  # 5% cancel mid-term
                    np.random.uniform(0.1, 0.9, N), 1.0)

# ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
# STEP 2: Build the True Risk Model
# These are the "true" relativities we want the GLM to recover
# ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

# Base frequency: 0.08 claims per policy-year (8% claim rate)
BASE_FREQ = 0.08

# Vehicle age relativities: U-shaped (new cars in accidents, old cars fail)
age_freq_rel = {0: 1.20, 1: 1.10, 2: 1.05, 3: 1.00, 4: 0.98,
                5: 0.97, 6: 0.99, 7: 1.02, 8: 1.05, 9: 1.10,
                10: 1.15, 11: 1.20, 12: 1.25, 13: 1.30, 14: 1.35, 15: 1.40}

# Segment relativities
seg_freq_rel  = {'Hatchback': 1.00, 'Sedan': 1.05, 'SUV': 1.18,
                 'Luxury': 0.90, 'EV': 1.25}
seg_sev_rel   = {'Hatchback': 1.00, 'Sedan': 1.40, 'SUV': 1.85,
                 'Luxury': 3.20, 'EV': 2.10}

# Driver age relativities: young <25 and old >65 are higher risk
def driver_age_rel(age):
    if age < 21: return 2.20
    elif age < 25: return 1.65
    elif age < 30: return 1.20
    elif age < 45: return 1.00
    elif age < 60: return 1.10
    else: return 1.35

# Region relativities: Metro roads → higher frequency
region_freq_rel = {'Metro': 1.35, 'Tier1': 1.10,
                   'Tier2': 1.00, 'Tier3': 0.85}
region_sev_rel  = {'Metro': 1.20, 'Tier1': 1.10,
                   'Tier2': 1.00, 'Tier3': 0.90}

# NCD: acts as both risk selection AND discount
ncd_freq_rel = {0: 1.30, 20: 1.05, 25: 1.00,
                35: 0.88, 45: 0.80, 50: 0.72}

# Channel: digital has higher adverse selection
channel_freq_rel = {'Agent': 0.95, 'Broker': 1.00,
                    'Digital': 1.18, 'Bancassurance': 0.90}

# ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
# STEP 3: Simulate Claim Frequency
# Poisson with μ = BASE_FREQ × product of all relativities × exposure
# ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

mu_freq = (BASE_FREQ
           * np.array([age_freq_rel[a] for a in vehicle_age])
           * np.array([seg_freq_rel[s] for s in vehicle_segment])
           * np.array([driver_age_rel(d) for d in driver_age])
           * np.array([region_freq_rel[r] for r in region])
           * np.array([ncd_freq_rel[n] for n in ncd_level])
           * np.array([channel_freq_rel[c] for c in channel])
           * exposure)

claim_count = np.random.poisson(mu_freq)

# ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
# STEP 4: Simulate Claim Severity (for policies with ≥1 claim)
# Gamma with mean driven by vehicle value, segment, region
# ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

BASE_SEV = 30000  # Base claim cost ₹30,000

# Gamma shape parameter κ = 1.5 (moderate dispersion in severities)
GAMMA_SHAPE = 1.5

mu_sev = (BASE_SEV
          * np.array([seg_sev_rel[s] for s in vehicle_segment])
          * np.array([region_sev_rel[r] for r in region])
          * (vehicle_value / 8.0) ** 0.55)  # IDV effect: concave (not linear)

# Generate total claim cost = sum of individual claims
total_claim_cost = np.zeros(N)
for i in range(N):
    if claim_count[i] > 0:
        # Each claim drawn from Gamma(shape, scale=mu_sev/shape)
        indiv_claims = np.random.gamma(
            GAMMA_SHAPE, mu_sev[i] / GAMMA_SHAPE, claim_count[i])
        total_claim_cost[i] = indiv_claims.sum()

# Add 2% large losses (>₹2L) — fat tail simulation
large_loss_mask = (total_claim_cost > 0) & (np.random.rand(N) < 0.02)
total_claim_cost[large_loss_mask] *= np.random.uniform(3, 8,
                                                        large_loss_mask.sum())

# ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
# STEP 5: Generate Current Charged Premium
# Current tariff uses simplified factors — deliberately imperfect
# ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

BASE_RATE = 3500  # ₹3,500 base OD premium (on ₹8L IDV)

# Simplified current tariff — flat vehicle age bands, 4 regions
veh_age_tariff = np.where(vehicle_age <= 3, 1.10,
                  np.where(vehicle_age <= 7, 1.00,
                  np.where(vehicle_age <= 12, 0.95, 0.90)))

region_tariff = np.array([{'Metro': 1.25, 'Tier1': 1.10,
                            'Tier2': 1.00, 'Tier3': 0.90}[r]
                           for r in region])

ncd_discount = np.array([{0: 1.00, 20: 0.80, 25: 0.75,
                           35: 0.65, 45: 0.55, 50: 0.50}[n]
                          for n in ncd_level])

od_premium = (BASE_RATE
              * (vehicle_value / 8.0) ** 0.65
              * veh_age_tariff * region_tariff * ncd_discount * exposure)

# Add pricing noise ±15% (underwriting discretion / rounding)
od_premium *= np.random.uniform(0.85, 1.15, N)
od_premium = od_premium.round(0)

# ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
# STEP 6: Introduce Data Quality Issues
# Realistic: ~5% missing, outliers, wrong dtypes
# ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

# 5% missing driver age (not always captured at policy inception)
driver_age_series = driver_age.astype(float)
driver_age_series[np.random.rand(N) < 0.05] = np.nan

# 3% missing vehicle value (IDV disputes / fleet policies)
vehicle_value_series = vehicle_value.copy()
vehicle_value_series[np.random.rand(N) < 0.03] = np.nan

# 1% impossible values: vehicle age = 99 (data entry error)
vehicle_age_series = vehicle_age.copy()
vehicle_age_series[np.random.rand(N) < 0.01] = 99

# Seasonality: monsoon months (Jun–Sep) have higher frequency
# Captured as accident_month variable
accident_month = np.random.choice(range(1, 13), N,
    p=[0.08, 0.07, 0.08, 0.08, 0.08, 0.10,
       0.10, 0.10, 0.09, 0.08, 0.07, 0.07])

# ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
# STEP 7: Assemble the DataFrame
# ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

df = pd.DataFrame({
    'policy_id':       [f'POL{i:07d}' for i in range(N)],
    'accident_year':    accident_year,
    'accident_month':   accident_month,
    'exposure':         exposure.round(4),
    'vehicle_age':      vehicle_age_series,
    'vehicle_segment':  vehicle_segment,
    'vehicle_value':    vehicle_value_series.round(0),
    'driver_age':       driver_age_series,
    'region':           region,
    'ncd_level':        ncd_level,
    'channel':          channel,
    'renewal_number':   renewal_number,
    'od_premium':       od_premium,
    'claim_count':      claim_count,
    'claim_cost':       total_claim_cost.round(0),
})

# Derived columns
df['has_claim']   = (df['claim_count'] > 0).astype(int)
df['loss_ratio']  = df['claim_cost'] / df['od_premium']
df['pure_prem_actual'] = df['claim_cost'] / df['exposure']
df['claim_freq']  = df['claim_count'] / df['exposure']

# Severity (only for policies with claims)
df['avg_severity'] = np.where(
    df['claim_count'] > 0,
    df['claim_cost'] / df['claim_count'], np.nan)

print(f"Dataset shape: {df.shape}")
print(f"Claim frequency: {df['claim_count'].sum() / df['exposure'].sum():.3f}")
print(f"Overall Loss Ratio: {df['claim_cost'].sum() / df['od_premium'].sum():.1%}")
print(f"Missing driver_age: {df['driver_age'].isna().sum():,}")
df.to_csv('motor_od_portfolio.csv', index=False)
print("Saved: motor_od_portfolio.csv")
Design Rationale — Key Decisions
  • Poisson claim counts: The standard actuarial assumption for rare, independent claims. We deliberately chose BASE_FREQ = 0.08 matching Indian OD market experience.
  • Gamma severity: Strictly positive, right-skewed — the correct distributional choice for individual claim costs. Shape parameter κ=1.5 produces moderate dispersion.
  • U-shaped vehicle age risk: New cars are in more accidents (inexperienced owners, higher speeds); very old cars fail mechanically. This non-linearity is a key interview discussion point.
  • IDV depreciation: IRDAI's IDV formula reduces sum insured by 5%/year — without this, the model overstates severity for older vehicles.
  • Digital channel uplift: Web aggregator selection effect is real and documentable — good candidates raise this proactively.
  • Current tariff imperfection: The charged premium uses simplified, outmoded factors — this creates the "rate inadequacy gap" the GLM will reveal.

def portfolio_summary(df):

        'Total Written Premium ₹': f"₹{df['od_premium'].sum():,.0f}",

        'Avg Severity ₹': f"₹{df[df['claim_count']>0]['avg_severity'].mean():,.0f}",

# ── 2. FREQUENCY ANALYSIS BY RATING FACTOR ───────────────
def freq_by_factor(df, factor, ax, title=None):
    """Exposure-weighted claim frequency by factor level"""
    grp = df.groupby(factor).agg(
        exposure=('exposure', 'sum'),
        claims=('claim_count', 'sum'),
        premium=('od_premium', 'sum'),
        cost=('claim_cost', 'sum')
    ).reset_index()
    grp['frequency']  = grp['claims'] / grp['exposure']
    grp['loss_ratio'] = grp['cost']   / grp['premium']
    
    # Bar chart for frequency, line for LR
    ax2 = ax.twinx()
    ax.bar(grp[factor].astype(str), grp['frequency'], color=NAVY, alpha=0.75, label='Frequency')
    ax2.plot(grp[factor].astype(str), grp['loss_ratio'], 'o-', color=GOLD, linewidth=2, label='LR')
    ax2.axhline(0.65, color='red', linestyle='--', alpha=0.5, label='Target LR')
    ax.set_xlabel(factor, fontsize=9); ax.set_ylabel('Claim Frequency', fontsize=9)
    ax2.set_ylabel('Loss Ratio', fontsize=9)
    ax.set_title(title or factor, fontsize=10, color=NAVY, fontweight='bold')
    plt.setp(ax.xaxis.get_majorticklabels(), rotation=30, ha='right', fontsize=8)
    return grp

Actuarial Methodology

Modelling Framework

The Two-Part Multiplicative Model

The standard actuarial approach to motor pricing decomposes the pure premium (expected claim cost per unit of exposure) into two components:

Pure Premium = Frequency × Severity
Where Frequency = E[N/exposure] and Severity = E[X | N ≥ 1]

Each component is modelled separately using a Generalised Linear Model (GLM). This separation is important because the drivers of frequency and severity are often different. For example, young drivers have higher frequency (more accidents) but not necessarily higher severity (accidents at lower speeds).

ComponentDistributionLink FunctionOffsetResponse Variable
FrequencyPoissonLoglog(exposure)Claim count N
SeverityGammaLogNoneAvg cost X per claim
CombinedTweedie (p≈1.5)Loglog(exposure)Total claim cost

Why Poisson for Frequency?

  • Claims are rare independent events — exactly the Poisson assumption.
  • The Poisson distribution is a member of the Exponential Dispersion Family (EDF), enabling GLM estimation via IRLS.
  • The log link produces multiplicative relativities — a natural structure for insurance rating factors (e.g., SUV gets 1.18× the base frequency).
  • The offset term log(exposure) adjusts for varying policy durations without adding a parameter.
  • If overdispersion is present (Var[N] >> E[N]), use quasi-Poisson (scale parameter φ > 1) or Negative Binomial GLM.

Why Gamma for Severity?

  • Claim costs are strictly positive — Gamma is defined on (0, ∞).
  • The Gamma distribution has a constant coefficient of variation — variance grows with mean², matching observed insurance data where high-cost claims have higher absolute variability.
  • The log link produces multiplicative factors, consistent with the frequency model.
  • The Gamma assumption is tested via residual analysis and Q-Q plots of deviance residuals.

Why Not Tweedie Directly?

Model Choice Rationale

The Tweedie distribution (Poisson-Gamma compound, power parameter p ≈ 1.5) can model total claim cost directly and handles zero-inflated data naturally. However, the two-part approach is preferred because: (1) it provides interpretable diagnostics for each component; (2) frequency and severity models can use different rating factors; (3) it enables separate monitoring of emerging frequency and severity trends — a key management reporting requirement.

GLM Assumptions

  1. Independence: Each policy is assumed to be an independent risk unit. Multi-policy households violate this — a known limitation.
  2. Mean function: E[Y|X] = g⁻¹(Xβ) where g = log link.
  3. Variance function: Var[Y|X] = φ·V(μ) where V(μ) = μ (Poisson) or μ² (Gamma).
  4. Linear predictor: The log of the expected response is linear in the parameters — each factor enters multiplicatively on the original scale.

Theory

Statistical Theory

1. Generalised Linear Models — Core Theory

A GLM generalises ordinary linear regression to allow response variables with non-Gaussian distributions. It consists of three components:

1. Random component: Y ~ EDF(μ, φ)
2. Systematic component: η = Xβ = β₀ + β₁x₁ + ... + βₚxₚ
3. Link function: g(μ) = η, i.e. μ = g⁻¹(η)
EDF = Exponential Dispersion Family | φ = dispersion parameter

2. Poisson GLM (Frequency Model)

For policy i with claim count Nᵢ and exposure eᵢ:

Nᵢ | xᵢ ~ Poisson(μᵢ · eᵢ)
log(μᵢ · eᵢ) = log(eᵢ) + β₀ + β₁x₁ᵢ + β₂x₂ᵢ + ...
⟹ log(μᵢ) = β₀ + Σⱼ βⱼxⱼᵢ
log(eᵢ) is the offset — constrains coefficient to 1 without estimation

The multiplicative structure on the natural scale is fundamental:

μᵢ = exp(β₀) × exp(β₁x₁ᵢ) × exp(β₂x₂ᵢ) × ...
Relativity for level k of factor j = exp(β̂ⱼₖ)
A GLM with log link automatically produces multiplicative rating factors

Parameter Estimation — IRLS

Parameters β are estimated by maximum likelihood via Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS). At each iteration t:

β̂⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ = (XᵀW⁽ᵗ⁾X)⁻¹ XᵀW⁽ᵗ⁾z⁽ᵗ⁾
Working response: zᵢ⁽ᵗ⁾ = η̂ᵢ + (yᵢ - μ̂ᵢ) · (dη/dμ)ᵢ
Weight: wᵢ = 1 / [V(μ̂ᵢ) · (dη/dμ)²ᵢ]
For Poisson log-link: V(μ) = μ, dη/dμ = 1/μ → wᵢ = μ̂ᵢ

3. Gamma GLM (Severity Model)

For policy i with claim cost Xᵢ (given Nᵢ ≥ 1):

Xᵢ | Nᵢ ≥ 1, xᵢ ~ Gamma(shape κ, scale μᵢ/κ)
E[Xᵢ] = μᵢ, Var[Xᵢ] = μᵢ²/κ
log(μᵢ) = β₀ + Σⱼ βⱼxⱼᵢ
κ is the Gamma shape parameter — estimated via maximum likelihood

4. Deviance and Goodness-of-Fit

The GLM equivalent of R² is the scaled deviance. For Poisson:

D(y, μ̂) = 2 Σᵢ [yᵢ log(yᵢ/μ̂ᵢ) - (yᵢ - μ̂ᵢ)]
Null Deviance D₀ = deviance of intercept-only model
Pseudo-R² = 1 - D(model) / D₀
Good frequency GLMs typically achieve Pseudo-R² of 5–15% in motor pricing

5. Information Criteria

AIC = -2 log L̂ + 2p
BIC = -2 log L̂ + p log(n)
Lower is better. Used for model selection between competing factor structures

6. Gini Coefficient (Model Discrimination)

The Gini coefficient measures how well the model ranks risks from good to bad. It is computed from the double-lift chart:

Gini = 2 × AUC - 1 = (Lorenz curve area above diagonal) / (total area)
Gini = 0 → random model | Gini = 1 → perfect discrimination
Typical motor pricing GLM achieves Gini 25–45% on frequency, 15–30% on severity

7. Statistical Tests for Factor Significance

TestWhen UsedDecision Rule
Wald test (z-score)Individual coefficient significance|z| > 1.96 → significant at 5%
Likelihood Ratio TestFactor-level joint significanceΔAIC > 2 → factor adds value
Chi-squared test on devianceFactor inclusion/exclusionp < 0.05 → retain factor
Variance Inflation FactorMulticollinearity checkVIF > 10 → collinearity concern

8. Credibility Weighting (Bühlmann-Straub)

Where a rating factor cell has thin data (e.g., EV segment with only 4,000 policies), the GLM estimate is blended with a prior using Bühlmann credibility:

μ̂ᵢ = Zᵢ · x̄ᵢ + (1 - Zᵢ) · μ̂ (grand mean)
Zᵢ = nᵢ / (nᵢ + k) where k = σ²between / σ²within
As exposure nᵢ → ∞, Zᵢ → 1 (full credibility). As nᵢ → 0, Zᵢ → 0 (prior)

# ── 1. LIFT CHART (Frequency Model) ──────────────────────
def lift_chart(df, pred_col, actual_col, weight_col, n_bands=10, title=''):
    """
    Sort by predicted value, group into n_bands equal-weight buckets,
    compare actual vs predicted in each band.
    A good model shows lift curve tracking the ideal diagonal.
    """
    df_sorted = df.sort_values(pred_col).copy()
    df_sorted['cum_weight'] = df_sorted[weight_col].cumsum()
    total_weight = df_sorted[weight_col].sum()
    df_sorted['band'] = pd.cut(
        df_sorted['cum_weight'] / total_weight,
        bins=np.linspace(0, 1, n_bands+1), labels=range(1, n_bands+1),
        include_lowest=True)
    
    lift = df_sorted.groupby('band').apply(lambda x: pd.Series({
        'actual':    (x[actual_col] / x[weight_col]).mean(),
        'predicted': (x[pred_col]).mean(),
    }))
    
    # Normalise to mean = 1 (relativities)
    lift['actual_rel']    = lift['actual']    / lift['actual'].mean()
    lift['predicted_rel'] = lift['predicted'] / lift['predicted'].mean()
    
    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 5))
    ax.plot(lift.index, lift['actual_rel'], 'o-', color=NAVY,
            linewidth=2, markersize=6, label='Actual')
    ax.plot(lift.index, lift['predicted_rel'], 's--', color=GOLD,
            linewidth=2, markersize=6, label='Predicted')
    ax.axhline(1, color='gray', linewidth=0.8, linestyle=':')
    ax.set_xlabel('Band (1=lowest risk, 10=highest risk)')
    ax.set_ylabel('Relativity')
    ax.set_title(title, color=NAVY, fontweight='bold')
    ax.legend(); plt.tight_layout()
    plt.savefig(f'lift_{title.lower().replace(" ","_")}.png', dpi=150)
    return lift

def double_lift(df, model_pred_col, current_prem_col, actual_col, n_bands=10):

# ── 3. GINI COEFFICIENT ──────────────────────────────────
def gini_coefficient(actual, predicted, weights=None):
    """
    Compute Gini from the Lorenz curve.
    Sort by predicted risk score. Cumulate actual losses.
    Gini = 2*(AUC of Lorenz) - 1
    Perfect model: Gini=1. Random model: Gini=0.
    """
    df_g = pd.DataFrame({'actual': actual, 'predicted': predicted})
    if weights is not None:
        df_g['weight'] = weights
    else:
        df_g['weight'] = 1
    df_g = df_g.sort_values('predicted')
    cum_weight  = df_g['weight'].cumsum()   / df_g['weight'].sum()
    cum_actual  = (df_g['actual'] * df_g['weight']).cumsum() / (df_g['actual'] * df_g['weight']).sum()
    
    # Trapezoid rule for area under Lorenz curve
    auc_lorenz = np.trapz(cum_actual, cum_weight)
    gini = 2 * auc_lorenz - 1
    
    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 6))
    ax.plot(cum_weight, cum_actual, color=NAVY, linewidth=2, label=f'Model (Gini={gini:.3f})')
    ax.plot([0,1],[0,1], color='gray', linewidth=1, linestyle='--', label='Random')
    ax.fill_between(cum_weight, cum_actual, cum_weight, alpha=0.15, color=GOLD)
    ax.set_xlabel('Cumulative share of exposure')
    ax.set_ylabel('Cumulative share of actual claims cost')
    ax.set_title('Lorenz Curve — Frequency Model', color=NAVY, fontweight='bold')
    ax.legend(); ax.set_aspect('equal')
    plt.tight_layout(); plt.savefig('gini_lorenz.png', dpi=150)
    return gini

def kfold_cv_poisson(df, formula, k=5):
    """Stratified k-fold CV for GLM — returns out-of-fold Gini per fold"""
    import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
    import statsmodels.api as sm
    
    kf = KFold(n_splits=k, shuffle=True, random_state=42)
    ginis = []
    
    for fold, (train_idx, val_idx) in enumerate(kf.split(df)):
        tr = df.iloc[train_idx].copy()
        va = df.iloc[val_idx].copy()
        model = smf.glm(formula, data=tr,
                         family=sm.families.Poisson()).fit(disp=0)
        va['pred'] = model.predict(va) / va['exposure']
        g = gini_coefficient(va['claim_count']/va['exposure'],
                             va['pred'], va['exposure'])
        ginis.append(g)
        print(f"  Fold {fold+1}: Gini = {g:.4f}")
    
    print(f"\n  Mean Gini: {np.mean(ginis):.4f} ± {np.std(ginis):.4f}")
    return ginis

A. Technical GLM Questions (20)

What is a GLM and why is it preferred over OLS regression for insurance claim data?
Ideal Answer: A GLM extends ordinary linear regression in two ways: (1) it allows the response variable to follow any distribution from the Exponential Dispersion Family (not just Gaussian), and (2) it uses a link function to model a nonlinear transformation of the mean. For insurance, OLS fails because: claim counts are non-negative integers (Poisson is appropriate), claim costs are strictly positive and right-skewed (Gamma), and variance is not constant. The log link in a Poisson or Gamma GLM produces multiplicative rating factors, which is the natural structure for insurance pricing — a multiplicative tariff.
Explain the role of the offset term in the Poisson frequency model. Why log(exposure)?
Ideal Answer: The offset allows us to model claim frequency (claims per policy-year) while using claim count as the response variable. We write: log(E[N]) = log(e) + Xβ, which is equivalent to log(E[N/e]) = Xβ. The offset log(e) is included in the linear predictor with a fixed coefficient of exactly 1 — it's not estimated. This ensures we model frequency (rate), not raw counts, and policies with partial exposure contribute correctly to the model.
What is overdispersion in a Poisson model? How do you detect and fix it?
Ideal Answer: Poisson assumes Var[N] = E[N] (equal mean and variance). Overdispersion is when the observed variance exceeds the mean — common in insurance because claim counts are heterogeneous (not all policies truly have the same underlying risk, even after conditioning on rating factors). Detection: compute the deviance/df ratio — if >1.5, overdispersion is likely. Also check dispersion parameter φ from a quasi-Poisson fit. Fix: (1) Quasi-Poisson GLM — same point estimates, inflated standard errors; (2) Negative Binomial GLM — explicit overdispersion parameter; (3) Add more variables to reduce unexplained heterogeneity. Important: overdispersion does not bias the coefficient estimates in Poisson — only standard errors are affected.
Why use Gamma for claim severity rather than lognormal?
Ideal Answer: Both are valid for severity. The key difference: (1) Gamma is in the Exponential Dispersion Family, enabling direct GLM estimation via IRLS and proper deviance calculation. Lognormal is not in EDF in the same way. (2) Gamma has constant CV (Var ∝ μ²), meaning relative dispersion is constant — reasonable for motor OD. Lognormal has constant variance on the log scale. (3) Gamma residuals have well-understood diagnostics. In practice, I'd fit both and compare AIC, then check deviance residuals Q-Q plots. The Inverse Gaussian is another alternative if the severity distribution has a heavier right tail.
How does IRLS estimate the GLM parameters?
Ideal Answer: Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares is the standard algorithm for GLM maximum likelihood. At each iteration, it: (1) Computes fitted values μ̂ from current β; (2) Constructs a working response z = η̂ + (y - μ̂)(dη/dμ); (3) Computes weights w = 1/[V(μ̂)(dη/dμ)²]; (4) Solves the weighted least squares problem β̂ = (XᵀWX)⁻¹XᵀWz; (5) Repeats until convergence. For Poisson log-link: V(μ)=μ and dη/dμ=1/μ, so w = μ̂. The algorithm typically converges in 5–10 iterations. Failure to converge suggests collinearity, sparse cells, or separation.
What is the difference between the null deviance and residual deviance? What is pseudo-R²?
Ideal Answer: Null deviance = deviance of the intercept-only model (just the grand mean). Residual deviance = deviance after fitting all covariates. Pseudo-R² = 1 - D(model)/D(null). It measures the proportion of deviance explained by the covariates. For motor frequency GLMs, 5–15% pseudo-R² is typical — not because the model is poor, but because individual claim randomness (Poisson noise) is irreducible. A frequency GLM with 20% pseudo-R² is actually very good. This is different from time series R² — many interviewers test whether candidates understand this nuance.
Walk me through how to add a new rating factor to the GLM. What tests would you use?
Ideal Answer: Step 1: One-way analysis — plot frequency/severity/LR by the candidate factor. Check that there's a plausible risk explanation. Step 2: Fit a model with the new factor and compare: (a) AIC reduction ≥2; (b) Likelihood Ratio Test p-value <0.05; (c) Wald tests on individual coefficients. Step 3: Check the relativities are intuitive and confidence intervals are narrow enough for use. Step 4: Check that adding the factor doesn't materially change other relativities (partial correlation), and that VIF stays below 10. Step 5: Validate on holdout — does the Gini improve? Business step: confirm the factor is legally permissible (e.g., IRDAI restrictions on gender) and commercially viable to collect.
What is a lift chart, and how do you interpret it?
Ideal Answer: A lift chart (or model decile plot) ranks policies by predicted risk, divides into deciles by exposure weight, and compares actual vs predicted loss within each decile. A well-calibrated model should show: actual closely tracks predicted across all bands — the two lines overlap. A model with good discrimination shows a steep gradient from low to high bands. If actual is flat but predicted rises steeply, the model is discriminating but poorly calibrated. If actual rises but predicted is flat, there's information in the data the model isn't capturing. The lift ratio = actual in top decile / actual in bottom decile is a quick summary — typically 3–8× for motor.
What is the Gini coefficient in the context of insurance pricing? How is it computed?
Ideal Answer: In insurance, the Gini coefficient measures the model's ability to rank risks from best to worst. Sort policies by predicted risk score; plot cumulative share of actual losses against cumulative share of exposure (the Lorenz curve). Gini = 2 × AUC(Lorenz) - 1. A random model has Gini = 0 (diagonal line). A perfect model has Gini = 1 (all losses concentrated in top exposure bucket). Motor frequency GLMs typically achieve Gini 25–40%. The Gini is a one-number discrimination summary — complement with the full lift chart for pattern. Note: Gini on the training set is biased upward vs test set; report test-set Gini.
How would you handle a rating factor with very sparse cells (e.g., EV with only 4,000 policies)?
Ideal Answer: Sparse cells lead to wide confidence intervals and unreliable relativities. Options: (1) Bühlmann credibility — blend GLM estimate with prior (market rate or overall mean), Z = n/(n+k). (2) Smoothing — use penalised regression (ridge/LASSO) to shrink sparse factor levels toward the mean. (3) Factor grouping — collapse levels with insufficient exposure into a single group. (4) Bayesian hierarchical model — put a prior on the factor level distribution. (5) Industry data — use market benchmarks or reinsurer experience as a prior for the EV class. For IRDAI regulatory purposes, option (1) or (3) is most defensible. I'd document the credibility weight and flag the EV relativity as provisional pending more data.

What is a pure premium and why is it a better basis for pricing than loss ratio alone?
Ideal Answer: Pure premium = expected claims cost per unit of exposure. It's better than loss ratio for two reasons: (1) It's exposure-normalised — a segment with more exposure naturally produces more premium and more claims; the loss ratio conflates volume and profitability. (2) The pure premium can be directly projected forward and loaded for expenses and profit to produce the indicated premium: Indicated Premium = Pure Premium / Target Loss Ratio. Loss ratios, by contrast, vary with the premium charged — which you're trying to set. Using loss ratio as both the diagnostic and the target creates circularity. Pure premium decouples claims from premium adequacy.
Explain how NCD creates endogeneity in the GLM. Should NCD be a rating factor?
Ideal Answer: NCD is endogenous because it's determined by prior claims history — the very thing we're trying to model. Policies at NCD0 have either (a) just started or (b) had a claim last year. Policies at NCD50 have been claim-free for 5+ years. So NCD partially proxies for risk quality, not just the discount structure. Including NCD in a prospective model is legitimate if the goal is to predict future claims given the current NCD level — because NCD is observed at the time of pricing. However, the relativity will be a mix of the true risk effect and the selection effect. I'd include NCD but flag that the relativity overstates the causal effect. For pricing, what matters is the predictive relationship, not the causal one.
How would you assess whether the GLM has created adverse selection risk?
Ideal Answer: Adverse selection occurs when our pricing refinement allows competitors to cherry-pick the good risks we've now priced down. Risk assessment: (1) Double lift chart — if the segments we've improved on (Hatchback Tier3) have high price elasticity, competitors will undercut us there. (2) New business vs renewal mix — if new business starts coming disproportionately from segments we've increased (SUVs), that's selection pressure. (3) Market benchmark comparison — if our improved prices are still above market on good risks, we haven't created the pricing conditions for adverse selection. (4) Distribution analysis — compare distribution of new business risk scores before and after repricing. Alert if risk score distribution shifts upward.
What does IRDAI's File & Use process require? How does the GLM fit in?
Ideal Answer: Under IRDAI's File & Use process for motor OD: (1) The insurer must file the revised rating manual and supporting actuarial basis with IRDAI before implementation. (2) The actuarial note must explain the methodology, data used, key assumptions, and limitations. (3) Rating factors must not include prohibited variables (gender, religion, caste). (4) IRDAI does not pre-approve — the insurer files and can implement after 30 days unless IRDAI objects. The GLM provides the statistical evidence base: deviance reduction, AIC improvement, lift charts, and a comparison of actual vs current tariff by segment. The relative simplicity of the GLM (vs ML) makes it easier to explain to the regulator and gives interpretable relativities for the rating manual.

C. Model Critique Questions (20)

What are the main limitations of the Poisson GLM for claim frequency modelling?
Ideal Answer: Five key limitations: (1) Overdispersion — Poisson constrains Var=Mean; real data often has Var > Mean due to unobserved heterogeneity. (2) Independence — assumes each policy is independent; household multi-policy effects and geographic clustering violate this. (3) Log-linear structure — assumes all factors enter multiplicatively; real interactions (e.g., SUV × Young Driver interaction may not be purely multiplicative) require explicit engineering. (4) Zero-inflation — some segments may have structurally more zeros than Poisson predicts (e.g., commercial fleets with strong risk management). (5) Time homogeneity — the model assumes the risk relativities are stable over the data window; trend changes (e.g., COVID) violate this. Mitigation: overdispersion → quasi-Poisson; zero-inflation → ZIP model; interactions → explicit interaction terms or ML.
How would you identify and deal with multicollinearity in the GLM?
Ideal Answer: Detection: (1) VIF (Variance Inflation Factor) > 10 indicates problematic collinearity. (2) Condition number of X matrix. (3) Large swings in coefficients when adding/removing a variable. Common sources in motor: vehicle_segment correlated with vehicle_value (SUVs cost more), engine_cc correlated with segment. Treatment: (1) Drop one of the correlated variables if the other captures the risk signal adequately. (2) PCA — combine correlated variables into uncorrelated components (interpretability suffers). (3) Ridge regression — penalise large coefficients. (4) Use domain knowledge — if engine_cc and segment both represent vehicle size/value, use only segment (more business-meaningful). Note: collinearity doesn't bias predictions; it inflates SE and makes individual coefficients unstable.
Your GLM shows a negative relativity for luxury vehicles on frequency. A business stakeholder says this must be wrong. How do you respond?
Ideal Answer: I'd validate the finding before defending it: (1) One-way check — does the raw data also show lower claim frequency for Luxury? If yes, the GLM is correctly capturing the data signal. (2) Controlled explanation — luxury vehicle owners typically drive less (chauffeur-driven, garaged), have more cautious driving behaviour, and live in higher-security areas. The lower frequency relativity despite higher severity relativity is consistent with real-world data in India and UK. (3) Check sample size — if only 6,000 luxury policies, the confidence interval may include 1.0 (i.e., not significantly different from base). (4) Partial effect — confirm other factors (Metro region, young driver) are being controlled for. The business stakeholder is likely anchoring on total loss ratio (high, due to severity) rather than frequency alone.
Compare GLM to XGBoost for insurance pricing. When would you use each?
Ideal Answer: GLM advantages: interpretable multiplicative relativities; regulator-defensible; direct output as tariff factors; well-understood confidence intervals; handles exposure offset natively; stable with moderate data. XGBoost advantages: captures non-linear interactions automatically; typically 10–20% better Gini; handles mixed data types without preprocessing; robust to outliers. Use GLM when: regulatory approval is needed (IRDAI File & Use); tariff must be manually implementable by underwriters; data is moderate (<500K policies). Use XGBoost when: predicting risk scores for portfolio management, fraud detection, or retention propensity (not direct pricing); large data available; transparency is not the primary constraint. Best practice: use XGBoost for EDA/insight generation, then approximate with a GLM for the rating manual. Or use both: GLM for the filed tariff, XGBoost as an underwriting score overlay.
What is the double-lift chart and why is it more important than the single-lift chart for repricing decisions?
Ideal Answer: The single-lift chart shows how well the model ranks risks. The double-lift chart compares the GLM's pricing signal to the current tariff. Sort policies by the ratio (GLM indicated premium / current premium). Low ratios = model thinks current tariff overprices; high ratios = model thinks current tariff underprices. Plot actual loss ratio by band. If the model is correct: LR should be LOW in band 1 (currently overpriced = good risks) and HIGH in band 10 (currently underpriced = bad risks). This confirms that repricing based on the GLM will improve risk selection — and quantifies the potential P&L benefit. It's critical for the business case: "if we reprice as indicated, the currently underpriced segment (band 10) will either be corrected or the business will be written away to competitors at a loss."

D. Advanced Actuarial Questions (20)

Explain Tweedie regression and when you would use it instead of the two-part model.
Ideal Answer: The Tweedie distribution with power parameter p ∈ (1,2) is a Poisson-Gamma compound — it handles the mass at zero (no claim) and the continuous positive component (claim cost) in a single model. When p=1: Poisson; p=2: Gamma; p=1.5 approximately matches typical motor insurance data. Advantages: single model, handles zeros naturally, closed-form for pure premium. Disadvantages: p must be estimated (cross-validate or profile likelihood); harder to interpret; can't separately diagnose frequency and severity components; monitoring is less granular. Use Tweedie when: you want a single-stage model for computational simplicity; data is already at the claim cost level (not counts available separately); quick modelling for portfolio management. Use two-part model when: regulatory submission needs separate factor justification; management wants separate frequency and severity monitoring; frequency and severity factors differ materially (usually true).
How would you incorporate telematics data into the GLM framework?
Ideal Answer: Telematics data (speed profiles, acceleration, braking, mileage) requires several steps before GLM inclusion: (1) Feature engineering — distil raw telematics into risk-relevant variables: mean speed, % of high-speed driving, harsh braking events per km, night-time driving %. (2) Dimensionality reduction — use PCA or clustering to create telematics risk scores if many features. (3) Variable selection — Lasso or stepwise selection on the telematics features vs GLM AIC. (4) Exposure recalibration — telematics gives actual km-based exposure, replacing policy-year exposure for the frequency offset. (5) Model update cycle — telematics data accumulates over the policy year; update the model quarterly using developing data with appropriate IBNR adjustment. Regulatory note: IRDAI has approved limited telematics use in India but not full UBI (Usage-Based Insurance) yet — check current guidelines.
What is the relationship between the GLM pricing model and the chain ladder reserving model?
Ideal Answer: Both are additive/multiplicative models applied to triangle/matrix data using similar mathematics. The chain ladder applies development factors across columns (development periods); the GLM applies relativities across rows (rating factors). More directly: the Poisson GLM is mathematically equivalent to the chain ladder when applied to the claims triangle — the "over-dispersed Poisson" chain ladder model (England and Verrall, 2002) is exactly a GLM with accident year and development year as factors and claims as Poisson response. This connection is useful because: (1) GLM diagnostics apply to the chain ladder; (2) you can combine pricing and reserving data; (3) the Bornhuetter-Ferguson method can be seen as a Bayesian update of the chain ladder GLM. Understanding this unification is a strong signal in advanced interviews.
How would you set the base premium from the GLM, and how does the target loss ratio enter?
Ideal Answer: The GLM gives relativities — the relative premium across segments. The base premium (the premium for the reference level of all factors) must be set separately. Process: (1) Compute the portfolio average pure premium from the GLM: PP̄ = Σᵢ (pred_freqᵢ × pred_sevᵢ × eᵢ) / Σᵢ eᵢ. (2) Choose the base premium such that the on-level portfolio premium reproduces this. (3) Indicated base = PP̄ / Target_LR. (4) Then scale all tariff factor cells using the relativities. (5) Verify: portfolio weighted average of all cells = indicated base. The target LR (65%) is a business decision — it must cover: expected claims (65%), expenses (25%), profit (10%). If the company has a reinsurance programme, the base premium must also recover the reinsurance cost. This calibration step is distinct from GLM estimation and is an important interview point.
Describe Bühlmann-Straub credibility and how it applies to a thin rating factor level.
Ideal Answer: Bühlmann-Straub credibility applies when different risk groups (factor levels) have varying volumes of exposure. The credibility-weighted estimate is: μ̂ᵢ = Zᵢ x̄ᵢ + (1-Zᵢ)μ̂, where Zᵢ = nᵢ/(nᵢ+k) and k = σ²(within groups)/σ²(between groups). For a thin EV cell with only 4,000 policies: estimate k from the wider portfolio. If k=5,000 and n=4,000, Z=4,000/9,000=44% — meaning only 44% weight on the observed EV rate; 56% on the portfolio average. As EV volume grows, Z approaches 1 and the GLM estimate fully dominates. Key insight: this is mathematically equivalent to a mixed model random effect — the GLM can implement Bühlmann credibility via a random intercept for the thin factor level. This is a graduate-level connection that impresses senior interviewers.

# Automated AvE monitoring with statistical tests
def ave_significance_test(actual_freq, pred_freq, exposure, threshold=0.05):
    """
    Two-sided z-test for whether AvE is significantly different from 1.0
    Under Poisson: SE(AvE) ≈ 1/sqrt(total_claims)
    """
    total_actual = (actual_freq * exposure).sum()
    total_pred   = (pred_freq * exposure).sum()
    ave = total_actual / total_pred
    se  = 1 / np.sqrt(total_actual)  # Poisson approximation
    z   = (ave - 1) / se
    p   = 2 * (1 - stats.norm.cdf(abs(z)))
    alert = (p < threshold) and (abs(ave - 1) > 0.05)
    print(f"AvE = {ave:.3f} | z = {z:.2f} | p = {p:.4f} | Alert: {alert}")
    return ave, z, p, alert
